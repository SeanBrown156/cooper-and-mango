# Combat Design

> **Creative-system source of truth for Cooper & Mango battle identity.**
>
> Cooper & Mango uses a classic turn-based JRPG foundation, but its defining
> combat layer is about managing animals rather than simply commanding party
> members. The player chooses deliberate actions on their own turn, then decides
> how much to let each animal follow its instincts during enemy actions.
>
> This document defines the intended combat feel and progression model. Runtime
> implementation contracts belong in `TECHNICAL_GAME_DESIGN.md` once a system is
> ready to build.

## Core battle identity

The battle loop should be readable like a classic Final Fantasy encounter while
feeling mechanically specific to a party of pets.

The central idea is:

> **On your turn, decide what your animals should do. On the enemy's turn,
> decide how much to let them be themselves.**

The combat system is built around three connected ideas:

- **Instinct** — species-shaped reactions that can be triggered by enemy actions;
- **Zoom** — short-term excitement generated by embracing instinct;
- **Companionship** — long-term bonds that change how party members react to and
  protect one another.

These should be treated as core battle pillars rather than optional side
systems.

## Base battle structure

Combat remains turn-based and command-driven. The visual target can stay close
in spirit to classic 16-bit Final Fantasy battles: party characters on one side,
enemies and oversized bosses on the other, with expressive battle animations.

The important change is what happens during the enemy turn.

1. The player takes normal deliberate turns using attacks, skills, items and
   other commands.
2. An enemy begins or performs an action.
3. That action may create an **Instinct opportunity** for one or more party
   members.
4. The player can allow one eligible animal to react, or hold them back.
5. The Instinct changes the unfolding enemy action in a way that can be useful,
   risky, or both.
6. Allowing the animal to follow its Instinct increases that animal's Zoom
   Meter.
7. At maximum Zoom, the animal enters **Zoomies** and temporarily behaves more
   autonomously according to its personality.

The enemy turn should therefore remain strategically engaging without becoming
a reflex or parry game.

## Instinct

Every animal has **Instinct** as a core combat trait. Instinct is broadly shaped
by species, then specialised by the individual character.

An Instinct is not simply another skill selected during the animal's normal
turn. It is a contextual reaction to something an enemy is doing.

The player is not ordering an animal to feel an impulse. The animal already
wants to react; the player's choice is whether to **let them**.

### Player-facing rule

Do not expose an abstract taxonomy such as `movement`, `noise`, `threat` or
`scent` unless later playtesting proves it necessary. These concepts may exist
internally as implementation tags, but the player should understand an Instinct
opportunity from the fiction and animation.

For example:

- an enemy starts charging across the room;
- Mango visibly perks up;
- the game offers **Pounce?**

The player should understand why the reaction is available without needing a
`MOVEMENT` icon.

### Tradeoffs are mandatory

Instincts should not be free bonus actions. A good Instinct changes the enemy's
behaviour while creating a meaningful downside, positional risk, targeting
change, resource cost, or future complication.

The interesting question is not "Should I take the free action?" but:

> **Is indulging this animal right now worth what it may cause?**

### Response limit

Current prototype direction: for a given enemy action, the player may allow
**one** eligible party member to use an Instinct response.

If multiple animals react, choosing between them becomes part of the tactical
problem. This prevents the enemy turn from turning into a chain of automatic
free attacks.

The player can always choose to suppress the reaction and do nothing.

## Equippable Instincts and progression

Instinct should also be a major progression system.

Species defines the broad Instinct family available to a character, but
characters can unlock and **equip new Instincts as they level up**. This allows
build choice without abandoning the animal fantasy.

A small equipped set is preferable to an ever-growing command list. The current
direction is approximately two or three equipped Instincts per character,
subject to playtesting.

Two animals of the same species can therefore share a biological foundation
while playing very differently because of personality and equipped Instincts.

### Cooper — dog Instinct family

Cooper's starting Instinct is strongly defined:

**Bark**

When an enemy performs an eligible action, Cooper may bark.

Potential upside:

- distract or interrupt an enemy;
- redirect the enemy's attention toward Cooper;
- protect a more vulnerable ally;
- alter an enemy's planned behaviour.

Potential downside:

- Cooper becomes the target;
- the redirected attack may be dangerous for him;
- some enemies may become more aggressive rather than less;
- attracting attention is not always desirable.

Allowing Cooper to bark increases his Zoom Meter.

Possible later dog Instincts include:

- **Protective Bark** — redirect an attack aimed at a companion and soften it;
- **Warning Bark** — stronger interruption with a larger Zoom increase;
- **Snarl** — intimidate or alter an approaching enemy rather than simply draw
  attention;
- **Join In** — a more excitable dog may react when another party member attacks.

These are directions, not a locked ability list.

### Mango — cat Instinct family

Mango's exact starting Instinct is not yet locked. The leading prototype is:

**Pounce**

When an enemy's action creates an irresistible opening, Mango can launch himself
at it during the enemy turn.

Potential upside:

- free counter-damage;
- interrupt or alter movement-based actions;
- knock an enemy or object off course;
- exploit an exposed part of a larger enemy.

Potential downside:

- Mango commits himself into danger;
- he may end the reaction exposed or separated from the party;
- the enemy's action may continue with Mango now directly in its path;
- some enemies may deliberately bait a pounce.

The important design shape is different from Cooper's:

- **Cooper's Instinct brings danger toward himself.**
- **Mango's likely Instinct sends himself toward danger.**

That contrast should be preserved even if Mango's final starting Instinct is
renamed or revised.

## Zoom Meter

Zoom represents how wound-up, stimulated and excited an animal becomes during
battle.

The core source of Zoom should be **embracing Instinct**, rather than simply
receiving damage like a conventional Limit Break.

Each time the player lets an animal follow an Instinct, that animal becomes more
Zoomy. High-risk or especially intense Instincts may generate more Zoom.

This creates a deliberate tension:

- Instinct reactions are useful now;
- allowing them repeatedly pushes the character toward losing control later.

### Zoomies

At maximum Zoom, the character enters **Zoomies**.

Zoomies should be powerful, funny and personality-driven, but not fully under
player control. The animal temporarily acts according to its own behavioural
rules rather than selecting a conventional player-directed super move.

The player's responsibility is therefore partially causal: if Cooper goes
completely feral at an inconvenient moment, it is because the player kept
indulging the behaviours that wound him up.

Rocky is the extreme expression of this system: an unusually powerful party
member whose default state is effectively permanent maximum Zoom, making him
high-impact and inherently unpredictable.

## Companionship

Companionship is a persistent relationship between two party members. It is
separate from Zoom:

- **Zoom** is short-term excitement inside a battle;
- **Companionship** is long-term trust and attachment built across the journey.

Party members gradually bond by travelling and fighting together, but the
system should reward meaningful behaviour as well as simple time in the same
party.

Companionship can grow faster when animals:

- protect one another;
- redirect danger away from one another;
- follow up on one another's actions;
- rescue an injured companion;
- use pair-specific cooperative behaviour.

Avoid reducing Companionship to generic bonuses such as `+5% damage when these
characters are together`.

The goal is for relationships to alter behaviour.

### Relationship reactions

As a pair grows closer, they can unlock new contextual reactions during battle.
These should express how those specific characters care for each other.

Example progression for Cooper and Mango:

- early in the story, an attack on Mango is simply Mango's problem;
- later, Cooper may unlock **Protect**, allowing him to react when Mango is
  targeted;
- at very high Companionship, rare automatic defensive or supportive reactions
  may occur because the bond is now deeply established.

Mango's companionship behaviour should still feel like Mango. He should not
become a generic paladin simply because the relationship gauge is high. His
care might instead appear through distraction, opportunistic follow-ups,
staying close to an injured Cooper, or attacking something that threatens him.

### Pair-specific identity

Companionship belongs to the **pair**, not just the individual character.
Different relationships should therefore unlock different behaviours.

A character may be protective of one companion, competitive with another, or
chaotically enthusiastic around a third. This gives party composition an
emotional as well as tactical meaning.

## Character model

A battle character can be understood through three layers:

1. **Species** — defines the broad Instinct family.
2. **Personality** — changes the risks, rewards and unique reactions of that
   individual animal.
3. **Equipped Instincts** — the player's chosen reaction build.

Companionship then adds a fourth, relational layer between characters rather
than inside a single character sheet.

This is preferred over relying exclusively on conventional JRPG classes. A dog
and a cat should play differently because they behave differently, not because
one has been arbitrarily assigned `Paladin` and the other `Thief`.

## Design principles

When adding or evaluating a combat mechanic, preserve these rules:

1. **Animal behaviour before RPG abstraction.** A mechanic should be easy to
   explain through something the animal wants to do.
2. **Enemy turns are playable decisions.** The player should remain mentally
   engaged without requiring twitch timing.
3. **Instinct has a tradeoff.** Never make contextual reactions obvious free
   bonuses.
4. **Zoom comes from indulgence.** The player creates their own future chaos by
   repeatedly allowing strong Instinct reactions.
5. **Relationships change behaviour.** Companionship should unlock acts of
   care, protection and cooperation rather than only numerical buffs.
6. **Species is a foundation, not a prison.** Shared Instinct families can be
   personalised through personality, progression and equipment.
7. **Keep the surface simple.** Hidden implementation tags are acceptable;
   exposing unnecessary stimulus categories or systems to the player is not.

## Prototype questions

The first combat prototype should answer these questions before the system grows:

- Is one Instinct response per enemy action enough to create interesting choice?
- How long should the response window remain open in a purely turn-based game?
- How clearly can enemy animation communicate why an Instinct is available
  without explicit stimulus icons?
- How quickly should Instinct use fill the Zoom Meter?
- How disruptive can Zoomies be before unpredictability becomes frustrating?
- Is Pounce the correct starting cat Instinct for Mango, or is there a stronger
  plus/minus behaviour that better represents him?
- How many equipped Instincts create meaningful builds without cluttering enemy
  turns?
- How visible should Companionship progression be to the player?

Do not expand this into a large catalogue of species abilities until the Cooper,
Mango and Mi-chan prototype proves the basic loop is fun.
